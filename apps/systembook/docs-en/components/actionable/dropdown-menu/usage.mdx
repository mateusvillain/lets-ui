## Organize the items

- **Sort by frequency of use**, with the most common actions at the top.
- **Group by purpose** and separate the groups with dividers.
- **Place destructive actions last**, separated from the others and with the `danger` variant.
- Limit the menu to about **seven items** per group. Beyond that, the person has to read everything to find what they want.

<DosDonts variant="do" title="Grouped items and the dangerous action isolated">
  "Edit", "Duplicate" and "Move", a divider, and then "Delete" in red. The separation prevents accidental clicks.
</DosDonts>

<DosDonts variant="dont" title="Long, unordered list">
  Fifteen loose items, with "Delete" in the middle. It is hard to scan and easy to get wrong.
</DosDonts>

## Write the items

- Use a **verb in the imperative** and, when it fits, the object: "Edit profile", "Copy link".
- Sentence case and no final punctuation.
- Be consistent: if one item is "Delete", do not use "Remove" in another menu for the same action.
- Items that open a dialog can end with an ellipsis ("Rename…"), indicating there is one more step.

## Shortcuts

Show the keyboard shortcut when there is one, to teach the person how to go faster. Shortcuts appear aligned to the right and use the [Shortcut](../../typography/shortcut/index.mdx) component.

## Submenus

Use submenus sparingly and **never more than one level**. Submenus hide options and are hard to use with a mouse and on touch screens. If you need more levels, rethink the organization.

## Disabled items

Keep a disabled item visible when the person may expect the action to exist, but it is not available right now. If the action never applies to the context, remove the item.

## Best practices

- Use the menu for **actions**, not for navigation.
- The trigger should indicate that it opens a menu. A "more options" Icon Button (three dots) or a button with an arrow is a good pattern.
- Give the trigger a clear name, like "Options" or "More actions".
